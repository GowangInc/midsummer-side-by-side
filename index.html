Act 1, Scene 1

Shakespeare Modern English

Enter Theseus, Hippolyta, and Philostrate, with others.

Theseus

Now, fair Hippolyta, our nuptial hour Draws on apace. Four happy days bring in Another moon. But, O, methinks how slow This old moon wanes! She lingers my desires Like to a stepdame or a dowager Long withering out a young man’s revenue.

Theseus

Now, lovely Hippolyta, our wedding day is coming soon. Four happy days will bring another moon. But the old moon seems so slow to fade! It lingers over my desires like a stepmother or a widow, slowly using up a young man’s inheritance.

Hippolyta

Four days will quickly steep themselves in night; Four nights will quickly dream away the time; And then the moon, like to a silver bow in heaven, shall behold the night Of our solemnities.

Hippolyta

Four days will quickly sink into night; four nights will quickly dream the time away. Then the moon, like a silver bow in the sky, will look down on the night of our celebration.

Theseus

Go, Philostrate, Stir up the Athenian youth to merriments. Awake the pert and nimble spirit of mirth. Turn melancholy forth to funerals; The pale companion is not for our pomp.

Theseus

Go, Philostrate. Get the young people of Athens ready to celebrate. Wake up the lively, nimble spirit of joy. Send sadness off to funerals; that pale companion doesn’t belong at our celebration.

Philostrate exits.

Theseus

Hippolyta, I wooed thee with my sword And won thy love doing thee injuries, But I will wed thee in another key, With pomp, with triumph, and with reveling.

Theseus

Hippolyta, I won you with my sword and made you love me by hurting you. But I’ll marry you in a different spirit: with ceremony, triumph, and celebration.

Enter Egeus and his daughter Hermia, and Lysander and Demetrius.

Egeus

Happy be Theseus, our renownèd duke!

Egeus

May Theseus, our famous duke, be blessed!

Theseus

Thanks, good Egeus. What’s the news with thee?

Theseus

Thank you, good Egeus. What news do you bring?

Egeus

Full of vexation come I, with complaint Against my child, my daughter Hermia.— Stand forth, Demetrius.—My noble lord, This man hath my consent to marry her.— Stand forth, Lysander.—And, my gracious duke, This man hath bewitched the bosom of my child.— Thou, thou, Lysander, thou hast given her rhymes And interchanged love tokens with my child. Thou hast by moonlight at her window sung With feigning voice verses of feigning love And stol’n the impression of her fantasy With bracelets of thy hair, rings, gauds, conceits, Knacks, trifles, nosegays, sweetmeats—messengers Of strong prevailment in unhardened youth. With cunning hast thou filched my daughter’s heart, Turned her obedience (which is due to me) To stubborn harshness.—And, my gracious duke, Be it so she will not here before your Grace Consent to marry with Demetrius, I beg the ancient privilege of Athens: As she is mine, I may dispose of her, Which shall be either to this gentleman Or to her death, according to our law Immediately provided in that case.

Egeus

I come full of anger, with a complaint against my child, my daughter Hermia. Stand forward, Demetrius. My noble lord, I agree that this man should marry her. Stand forward, Lysander. And, gracious duke, this man has bewitched my daughter’s heart. You, Lysander—you’ve given her poems and exchanged love tokens with her. By moonlight, you’ve sung at her window in a fake voice, singing fake love poems. You’ve stolen her imagination with hair bracelets, rings, trinkets, clever gifts, little toys, flowers, and sweets—powerful messengers to young people whose hearts aren’t hardened yet. By your tricks, you’ve stolen my daughter’s heart and turned her obedience to me into stubborn defiance. And, gracious duke, if she won’t agree here before you to marry Demetrius, I ask for Athens’ ancient right: since she belongs to me, I may decide her fate. Under our law, she must either marry this gentleman or die.

Theseus

What say you, Hermia? Be advised, fair maid. To you, your father should be as a god, One that composed your beauties, yea, and one To whom you are but as a form in wax By him imprinted, and within his power To leave the figure or disfigure it. Demetrius is a worthy gentleman.

Theseus

What do you say, Hermia? Think carefully, young woman. To you, your father should be like a god: he shaped your beauty, and you are like wax that he formed. He has the power to leave you as you are or change your shape. Demetrius is a worthy gentleman.

Hermia

So is Lysander.

Hermia

So is Lysander.

Theseus

In himself he is, But in this kind, wanting your father’s voice, The other must be held the worthier.

Theseus

He is worthy in himself. But since your father doesn’t approve of him, the other man must be considered more worthy.

Hermia

I would my father looked but with my eyes.

Hermia

I wish my father could see things with my eyes.

Theseus

Rather your eyes must with his judgment look.

Theseus

Instead, your eyes must see things with his judgment.

Hermia

I do entreat your Grace to pardon me. I know not by what power I am made bold, Nor how it may concern my modesty In such a presence here to plead my thoughts; But I beseech your Grace that I may know The worst that may befall me in this case If I refuse to wed Demetrius.

Hermia

Please forgive me, Your Grace. I don’t know what gives me the courage to speak, or how it might affect my modesty to plead my thoughts here in front of everyone. But please tell me the worst that can happen if I refuse to marry Demetrius.

Theseus

Either to die the death or to abjure Forever the society of men. Therefore, fair Hermia, question your desires, Know of your youth, examine well your blood, Whether (if you yield not to your father’s choice) You can endure the livery of a nun, For aye to be in shady cloister mewed, To live a barren sister all your life, Chanting faint hymns to the cold fruitless moon. Thrice-blessèd they that master so their blood To undergo such maiden pilgrimage, But earthlier happy is the rose distilled Than that which, withering on the virgin thorn, Grows, lives, and dies in single blessedness.

Theseus

Either you die or you must leave human society forever. So, fair Hermia, think about what you want. Consider your youth and examine your feelings. If you refuse your father’s choice, can you bear the life of a nun—shut away forever in a dark convent, living as a sister without children all your life, singing weak hymns to the cold, barren moon? Those who control their desires enough to endure that life are three times blessed. But a rose that is distilled into perfume is happier on earth than one that withers on the thorn, living and dying alone in blessed purity.

Hermia

So will I grow, so live, so die, my lord, Ere I will yield my virgin patent up Unto his Lordship whose unwishèd yoke My soul consents not to give sovereignty.

Hermia

Then I’ll grow old, live, and die unmarried, my lord. I won’t give Demetrius power over me by marrying him against my will.

Theseus

Take time to pause, and by the next new moon (The sealing day betwixt my love and me For everlasting bond of fellowship), Upon that day either prepare to die For disobedience to your father’s will, Or else to wed Demetrius, as he would, Or on Diana’s altar to protest For aye austerity and single life.

Theseus

Take time to think. By the next new moon—the day my love and I will seal our lasting bond—you must either be ready to die for disobeying your father, marry Demetrius as he wishes, or swear at Diana’s altar to live a strict, unmarried life forever.

Demetrius

Relent, sweet Hermia, and, Lysander, yield Thy crazèd title to my certain right.

Demetrius

Give in, sweet Hermia, and Lysander, give up your crazy claim to what rightfully belongs to me.

Lysander

You have her father’s love, Demetrius. Let me have Hermia’s. Do you marry him.

Lysander

You have her father’s love, Demetrius. Let me have Hermia’s. You marry him.

Egeus

Scornful Lysander, true, he hath my love; And what is mine my love shall render him. And she is mine, and all my right of her I do estate unto Demetrius.

Egeus

You can mock me, Lysander, but Demetrius is the man I favour. I’ll give him what is mine to give. Hermia is mine, and I pass all my rights over her to Demetrius.

To Theseus

Lysander

I am, my lord, as well derived as he, As well possessed. My love is more than his; My fortunes every way as fairly ranked (If not with vantage) as Demetrius’; And (which is more than all these boasts can be) I am beloved of beauteous Hermia. Why should not I then prosecute my right? Demetrius, I’ll avouch it to his head, Made love to Nedar’s daughter, Helena, And won her soul; and she, sweet lady, dotes, Devoutly dotes, dotes in idolatry, Upon this spotted and inconstant man.

Lysander

My family background is as good as his, my position just as secure. My love is greater than his, and my wealth is at least as well matched to his, if not better. More than all those claims, beautiful Hermia loves me. Why shouldn’t I stand up for my rights? Demetrius, I’ll say this to your face: you courted Nedar’s daughter, Helena, and won her heart. And she, that sweet lady, adores you—adores you devoutly, worships you like a god—though you’re a faithless, fickle man.

Theseus

I must confess that I have heard so much, And with Demetrius thought to have spoke thereof; But, being overfull of self-affairs, My mind did lose it.—But, Demetrius, come, And come, Egeus; you shall go with me. I have some private schooling for you both.— For you, fair Hermia, look you arm yourself To fit your fancies to your father’s will, Or else the law of Athens yields you up (Which by no means we may extenuate) To death or to a vow of single life.— Come, my Hippolyta. What cheer, my love?— Demetrius and Egeus, go along. I must employ you in some business Against our nuptial and confer with you Of something nearly that concerns yourselves.

Theseus

I have to admit I’ve heard about it, and I meant to speak to Demetrius about it. But I was so wrapped up in my own affairs that I forgot. Demetrius, come with me. Egeus, come too. I have some private advice for you both. Hermia, make yourself ready to obey your father’s wishes, or Athenian law will hand you over—there’s no way we can soften it—to death or a life of devotion and no marriage. Come, my Hippolyta. How are you, my love? Demetrius and Egeus, come along. I have some business to give you, connected with our wedding, and I need to discuss something that concerns you both.

Egeus

With duty and desire we follow you.

Egeus

We follow you with respect and willingness.

All but Hermia and Lysander exit.

Lysander

How now, my love? Why is your cheek so pale? How chance the roses there do fade so fast?

Lysander

What’s wrong, my love? Why is your cheek so pale? Why have the roses in it faded so quickly?

Hermia

Belike for want of rain, which I could well Beteem them from the tempest of my eyes.

Hermia

Perhaps they’ve had no rain, though I could easily water them with the storm in my eyes.

Lysander

Ay me! For aught that I could ever read, Could ever hear by tale or history, The course of true love never did run smooth. But either it was different in blood—

Lysander

Oh, dear! From everything I’ve ever read or heard in stories and history, true love never runs smoothly. Sometimes the lovers are of different ranks—

Hermia

O cross! Too high to be enthralled to low.

Hermia

Oh, no! One is too high-born to be tied to someone low-born.

Lysander

Or else misgraffèd in respect of years—

Lysander

Or they’re badly matched in age—

Hermia

O spite! Too old to be engaged to young.

Hermia

Oh, how unfair! One is too old to be engaged to someone young.

Lysander

Or else it stood upon the choice of friends—

Lysander

Or their friends choose for them—

Hermia

O hell, to choose love by another’s eyes!

Hermia

Oh, hell, when someone else gets to choose whom you love!

Lysander

Or, if there were a sympathy in choice, War, death, or sickness did lay siege to it, Making it momentany as a sound, Swift as a shadow, short as any dream, Brief as the lightning in the collied night, That, in a spleen, unfolds both heaven and Earth, And, ere a man hath power to say “Behold!” The jaws of darkness do devour it up. So quick bright things come to confusion.

Lysander

Or, even when the lovers choose each other, war, death, or sickness attacks their love, making it as brief as a sound, as quick as a shadow, as short as any dream, as brief as lightning on a dark night. In a flash, lightning opens up heaven and earth, and before anyone can say, “Look!” darkness swallows it. So quickly bright things fall apart.

Hermia

If then true lovers have been ever crossed, It stands as an edict in destiny. Then let us teach our trial patience Because it is a customary cross, As due to love as thoughts and dreams and sighs, Wishes and tears, poor fancy’s followers.

Hermia

If true lovers have always faced obstacles, then it must be fate’s decree. So let’s be patient through our troubles, because this is a common hardship, as much a part of love as thoughts, dreams, sighs, wishes, and tears—the followers of longing.

Lysander

A good persuasion. Therefore, hear me, Hermia: I have a widow aunt, a dowager Of great revenue, and she hath no child. From Athens is her house remote seven leagues, And she respects me as her only son. There, gentle Hermia, may I marry thee; And to that place the sharp Athenian law Cannot pursue us. If thou lovest me, then Steal forth thy father’s house tomorrow night, And in the wood a league without the town (Where I did meet thee once with Helena To do observance to a morn of May), There will I stay for thee.

Lysander

That’s a good way to look at it. So listen, Hermia: I have a widowed aunt, a rich woman whose husband has died, and she has no children. Her house is seven leagues from Athens, and she treats me as her only son. There, dear Hermia, I can marry you. Athenian law can’t reach us there. If you love me, slip out of your father’s house tomorrow night. I’ll wait for you in the wood, a league outside town, where I once met you and Helena to celebrate the May morning.

Hermia

My good Lysander, I swear to thee by Cupid’s strongest bow, By his best arrow with the golden head, By the simplicity of Venus’ doves, By that which knitteth souls and prospers loves, And by that fire which burned the Carthage queen When the false Trojan under sail was seen, By all the vows that ever men have broke (In number more than ever women spoke), In that same place thou hast appointed me, Tomorrow truly will I meet with thee.

Hermia

My dear Lysander, I swear to you by Cupid’s strongest bow, by his best golden-tipped arrow, by Venus’s innocent doves, by the power that joins hearts and makes love thrive, and by the fire that burned the queen of Carthage when she saw the false Trojan sail away. I swear by all the promises men have ever broken—more than women have ever spoken—that I will truly meet you tomorrow in the place you named.

Lysander

Keep promise, love. Look, here comes Helena.

Lysander

Keep your promise, my love. Look, here comes Helena.

Enter Helena.

Hermia

Godspeed, fair Helena. Whither away?

Hermia

Hello, lovely Helena. Where are you going?

Helena

Call you me “fair”? That “fair” again unsay. Demetrius loves your fair. O happy fair! Your eyes are lodestars and your tongue’s sweet air More tunable than lark to shepherd’s ear When wheat is green, when hawthorn buds appear. Sickness is catching. O, were favor so! Yours would I catch, fair Hermia, ere I go. My ear should catch your voice, my eye your eye; My tongue should catch your tongue’s sweet melody. Were the world mine, Demetrius being bated, The rest I’d give to be to you translated. O, teach me how you look and with what art You sway the motion of Demetrius’ heart!

Helena

You call me beautiful? Take that back. Demetrius loves your beauty. Lucky you! Your eyes are guiding stars, and your voice is sweeter and more musical than a lark singing to a shepherd when the wheat is green and the hawthorn is budding. Sickness spreads by catching. If beauty worked that way, I’d catch yours before I go, fair Hermia. My ears would catch your voice, my eyes your eyes, my tongue your tongue’s sweet music. If the world were mine, except for Demetrius, I’d give it all to become like you. Oh, teach me how you look and what you do to control Demetrius’ heart!

Hermia

I frown upon him, yet he loves me still.

Hermia

I frown at him, but he still loves me.

Helena

O, that your frowns would teach my smiles such skill!

Helena

Oh, if your frowns could teach my smiles that skill!

Hermia

I give him curses, yet he gives me love.

Hermia

I curse him, but he gives me love.

Helena

O, that my prayers could such affection move!

Helena

Oh, if my prayers could stir up love like that!

Hermia

The more I hate, the more he follows me.

Hermia

The more I hate him, the more he follows me.

Helena

The more I love, the more he hateth me.

Helena

The more I love him, the more he hates me.

Hermia

His folly, Helena, is no fault of mine.

Hermia

His foolishness isn’t my fault, Helena.

Helena

None but your beauty. Would that fault were mine!

Helena

Your beauty is the only fault. I wish it were mine!

Hermia

Take comfort: he no more shall see my face. Lysander and myself will fly this place. Before the time I did Lysander see Seemed Athens as a paradise to me. O, then, what graces in my love do dwell That he hath turned a heaven unto a hell!

Hermia

Take heart: he won’t see my face again. Lysander and I are leaving. Before I met him, Athens seemed like heaven to me. What must be so wonderful about Lysander that loving him has turned this heaven into hell?

Lysander

Helen, to you our minds we will unfold. Tomorrow night when Phoebe doth behold Her silver visage in the wat’ry glass, Decking with liquid pearl the bladed grass (A time that lovers’ flights doth still conceal), Through Athens’ gates have we devised to steal.

Lysander

Helena, we’ll tell you our plan. Tomorrow night, when Phoebe, the moon, sees her silver face in the water and hangs drops of dew like pearls on the grass, we’ll slip out through the gates of Athens. That’s the hour that hides lovers on the run.

Hermia

And in the wood where often you and I Upon faint primrose beds were wont to lie, Emptying our bosoms of their counsel sweet, There my Lysander and myself shall meet And thence from Athens turn away our eyes To seek new friends and stranger companies. Farewell, sweet playfellow. Pray thou for us, And good luck grant thee thy Demetrius.— Keep word, Lysander. We must starve our sight From lovers’ food till morrow deep midnight.

Hermia

And in the wood where you and I often lay on soft beds of primroses, sharing our secrets, there Lysander and I will meet. From there, we’ll turn our backs on Athens and seek new friends and strangers. Farewell, dear friend. Please pray for us, and may good luck bring you your Demetrius. Keep your promise, Lysander. We must go without seeing one another until well after midnight tomorrow.

Lysander

I will, my Hermia.

Lysander

I will, my Hermia.

Hermia exits.

Lysander

Helena, adieu. As you on him, Demetrius dote on you!

Lysander

Goodbye, Helena. May Demetrius love you as you love him!

Lysander exits.

Helena

How happy some o’er other some can be! Through Athens I am thought as fair as she. But what of that? Demetrius thinks not so. He will not know what all but he do know. And, as he errs, doting on Hermia’s eyes, So I, admiring of his qualities. Things base and vile, holding no quantity, Love can transpose to form and dignity. Love looks not with the eyes but with the mind; And therefore is winged Cupid painted blind. Nor hath Love’s mind of any judgment taste. Wings, and no eyes, figure unheedy haste. And therefore is Love said to be a child Because in choice he is so oft beguiled. As waggish boys in game themselves forswear, So the boy Love is perjured everywhere. For, ere Demetrius looked on Hermia’s eyne, He hailed down oaths that he was only mine; And when this hail some heat from Hermia felt, So he dissolved, and show’rs of oaths did melt. I will go tell him of fair Hermia’s flight. Then to the wood will he tomorrow night Pursue her. And, for this intelligence If I have thanks, it is a dear expense. But herein mean I to enrich my pain, To have his sight thither and back again.

Helena

Some people are so much happier than others! In Athens, people think I’m as beautiful as she is. But what does that matter? Demetrius doesn’t think so. He refuses to see what everyone else sees. Just as he’s mistaken, adoring Hermia’s eyes, I’m mistaken, admiring his fine qualities. Love can turn worthless, ugly things into something beautiful and grand. Love doesn’t look with the eyes, but with the mind. That’s why Cupid is shown with a blindfold. And Love’s mind has no sense of judgment. Wings and no eyes stand for reckless haste. That’s why Love is said to be a child: he’s so often fooled by his choices. Like mischievous boys who break their promises in games, the boy Love breaks his promises everywhere. Before Demetrius looked into Hermia’s eyes, he swore again and again he was only mine. But when Hermia’s beauty warmed those oaths, they melted like snow, and his promises poured away. I’ll go tell him beautiful Hermia is running away. Then tomorrow night he’ll follow her into the wood. If he thanks me for telling him, I’ll pay dearly for it. But here’s how I’ll turn my pain into a gain: I’ll get to see him there and back again.

She exits.

Shakespeare Modern English

Enter Quince the carpenter, and Snug the joiner, and Bottom the weaver, and Flute the bellows-mender, and Snout the tinker, and Starveling the tailor.

Quince

Is all our company here?

Quince

Is everyone in our company here?

Bottom

You were best to call them generally, man by man, according to the scrip.

Bottom

You’d better call them all, one by one, according to the list.

Quince

Here is the scroll of every man’s name which is thought fit, through all Athens, to play in our interlude before the Duke and the Duchess on his wedding day at night.

Quince

Here’s the list of every man in Athens thought fit to act in our play before the Duke and Duchess on their wedding night.

Bottom

First, good Peter Quince, say what the play treats on, then read the names of the actors, and so grow to a point.

Bottom

First, good Peter Quince, tell us what the play is about, then read out the actors’ names, and get to the point.

Quince

Marry, our play is The most lamentable comedy and most cruel death of Pyramus and Thisbe.

Quince

Indeed, our play is called The Most Lamentable Comedy and Most Cruel Death of Pyramus and Thisbe.

Bottom

A very good piece of work, I assure you, and a merry. Now, good Peter Quince, call forth your actors by the scroll. Masters, spread yourselves.

Bottom

A very good piece of work, I assure you, and a funny one. Now, good Peter Quince, call out your actors from the list. Gentlemen, spread out.

Quince

Answer as I call you. Nick Bottom, the weaver.

Quince

Answer when I call you. Nick Bottom, the weaver.

Bottom

Ready. Name what part I am for, and proceed.

Bottom

Ready. Tell me what part I’m playing, and carry on.

Quince

You, Nick Bottom, are set down for Pyramus.

Quince

You, Nick Bottom, are listed to play Pyramus.

Bottom

What is Pyramus—a lover or a tyrant?

Bottom

What is Pyramus—a lover or a tyrant?

Quince

A lover that kills himself most gallant for love.

Quince

A lover who kills himself very bravely for love.

Bottom

That will ask some tears in the true performing of it. If I do it, let the audience look to their eyes. I will move storms; I will condole in some measure. To the rest.—Yet my chief humor is for a tyrant. I could play Ercles rarely, or a part to tear a cat in, to make all split:

Bottom

That will take some tears to perform properly. If I play it, the audience had better watch their eyes. I’ll stir up storms of emotion; I’ll mourn quite well. On to the others.—Still, my real strength is playing a tyrant. I could play Hercules brilliantly, or a part that would have everyone in stitches:

Bottom

The raging rocks And shivering shocks Shall break the locks Of prison gates. And Phibbus’ car Shall shine from far And make and mar The foolish Fates.

Bottom

“Raging rocks and crashing shocks will smash the locks of prison doors. And the sun god’s chariot will blaze from far away, making and breaking whatever the foolish Fates decide.”

Bottom

This was lofty. Now name the rest of the players. This is Ercles’ vein, a tyrant’s vein. A lover is more condoling.

Bottom

That was grand. Now name the rest of the players. That’s Hercules’ style, a tyrant’s style. A lover is more mournful.

Quince

Francis Flute, the bellows-mender.

Quince

Francis Flute, the bellows-mender.

Flute

Here, Peter Quince.

Flute

Here I am, Peter Quince.

Quince

Flute, you must take Thisbe on you.

Quince

Flute, you must play Thisbe.

Flute

What is Thisbe—a wand’ring knight?

Flute

What is Thisbe—a wandering knight?

Quince

It is the lady that Pyramus must love.

Quince

It’s the woman Pyramus must love.

Flute

Nay, faith, let not me play a woman. I have a beard coming.

Flute

No, honestly, don’t make me play a woman. I’m growing a beard.

Quince

That’s all one. You shall play it in a mask, and you may speak as small as you will.

Quince

Makes no difference. The mask will cover your face, so speak in as tiny a voice as you please.

Bottom

An I may hide my face, let me play Thisbe too. I’ll speak in a monstrous little voice: “Thisne, Thisne!”—“Ah Pyramus, my lover dear! Thy Thisbe dear and lady dear!”

Bottom

If I can hide my face, let me play Thisbe too. I’ll use a very tiny voice: “Thisne, Thisne!”—“Ah, Pyramus, my dear love! Your dear Thisbe, your dear lady!”

Quince

No, no, you must play Pyramus—and, Flute, you Thisbe.

Quince

No, no. You must play Pyramus—and, Flute, you play Thisbe.

Bottom

Well, proceed.

Bottom

All right, carry on.

Quince

Robin Starveling, the tailor.

Quince

Robin Starveling, the tailor.

Starveling

Here, Peter Quince.

Starveling

Here, Peter Quince.

Quince

Robin Starveling, you must play Thisbe’s mother.—Tom Snout, the tinker.

Quince

Robin Starveling, you must play Thisbe’s mother. Tom Snout, the mender of pots and pans.

Snout

Here, Peter Quince.

Snout

Here, Peter Quince.

Quince

You, Pyramus’ father.—Myself, Thisbe’s father.—Snug the joiner, you the lion’s part.—And I hope here is a play fitted.

Quince

You play Pyramus’s father. I’ll play Thisbe’s father. Snug the joiner, you play the lion. And I hope we have a play that’s just right.

Snug

Have you the lion’s part written? Pray you, if it be, give it me, for I am slow of study.

Snug

Have you written out the lion’s part? Please give it to me if you have, because I’m slow at learning lines.

Quince

You may do it extempore, for it is nothing but roaring.

Quince

You can make it up as you go, because all you have to do is roar.

Bottom

Let me play the lion too. I will roar that I will do any man’s heart good to hear me. I will roar that I will make the Duke say “Let him roar again. Let him roar again!”

Bottom

Let me play the lion too. I’ll roar so well that anyone who hears me will feel good. I’ll roar so loudly the Duke will say, “Let him roar again! Let him roar again!”

Quince

An you should do it too terribly, you would fright the Duchess and the ladies that they would shriek, and that were enough to hang us all.

Quince

If you do it too terrifyingly, you’ll frighten the Duchess and the ladies into screaming, and that would be enough to get us all hanged.

All

That would hang us, every mother’s son.

All
